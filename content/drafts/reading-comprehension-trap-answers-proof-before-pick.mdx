## What the proof-before-pick method means

A **reading comprehension trap answer** is not always absurd. It may sound intelligent, repeat a phrase from the passage, or express something that is true in everyday life. It becomes wrong when the passage and the question do not justify it.

The **proof-before-pick method** turns answer selection into an auditable process:

1. Identify exactly what the question asks.
2. Check whether the option contradicts the passage.
3. Check whether it stays within the passage’s scope.
4. Demand evidence for every important part of the option.
5. Measure how far the option moves beyond the text.
6. Inspect precise wording, including absolute and comparative terms.

You do not need a verbatim sentence to prove every answer. Some questions require a reasonable inference. The key is that the inference must be controlled by the passage rather than by outside assumptions.

The examples in this article are original and illustrative. They are designed to demonstrate the validation process, not to reproduce questions from any examination.

## Step 1: Identify exactly what the question asks

Before examining the choices, classify the task. A main-idea question asks about the passage as a whole. A detail question asks what the passage states. An inference question asks what follows from the text. A tone question asks about the author’s attitude. An application question asks you to transfer the passage’s principle to a new situation.

A choice can be true somewhere in the passage and still fail because it answers a different question.

### Worked example: question mismatch

**Passage:**

> Urban tree-planting programmes are often evaluated by the number of saplings planted. That measure is easy to record, but it does not reveal whether the trees survive, receive adequate water, or benefit neighbourhoods most affected by heat. A better evaluation would track survival and local temperature over several years.

**Question:** What is the main criticism made in the passage?

| Option | Proof check |
|---|---|
| A. Urban tree-planting programmes should be cancelled. | Fails. The passage criticises an evaluation method, not the programmes themselves. It also introduces cancellation. |
| B. Counting planted saplings is insufficient for evaluating the programmes’ success. | Passes. It states the central criticism without adding a policy conclusion. |

**Passage claim:** Counting saplings is too narrow; evaluation should also consider survival and impact.

**Correct choice:** B. Option A answers a nearby policy question—what should be done—instead of the question asked.

When two choices seem plausible, ask: **Which one answers this exact question, rather than a related question?**

## Step 2: Test for direct contradiction

Contradiction is often the quickest reason to reject an option. Compare the choice with the passage’s actual claim, not with your memory of the topic.

Look for reversals such as:

- increased versus decreased;
- some versus all;
- a possible explanation versus an established cause;
- criticism versus support;
- earlier versus later.

### Worked example: contradiction

**Passage:**

> The museum’s new audio guide attracted younger visitors, but the survey did not show that these visitors stayed longer than other guests. The guide may have encouraged attendance without changing the length of each visit.

**Question:** What does the survey establish?

| Option | Proof check |
|---|---|
| A. The audio guide caused younger visitors to spend more time in the museum. | Fails. The passage says longer visits were not demonstrated and does not establish causation. |
| B. The audio guide was associated with greater attendance among younger visitors, but its effect on visit length was not established. | Passes. It preserves both the supported result and the limitation. |

**Correct choice:** B. Option A is attractive because it connects the guide with younger visitors, but it reverses the passage’s qualification. Read every clause; one familiar phrase does not rescue a contradictory conclusion.

## Step 3: Check whether the option stays within scope

Scope concerns the boundaries of a claim. A choice becomes too broad when it extends a statement from:

- one group to everyone;
- one location to all locations;
- one period to all periods;
- one example to a general rule;
- one factor to an entire explanation.

### Worked example: scope expansion

**Passage:**

> In a study of three coastal villages, households that repaired roofs before the monsoon reported fewer interruptions to home-based work. The researchers noted that roof quality was only one factor affecting whether people could continue working during storms.

**Question:** What conclusion is best supported?

| Option | Proof check |
|---|---|
| A. Roof repairs ensure that all coastal households can work normally during storms. | Fails. It expands the population, turns an association into a guarantee, and ignores other factors. |
| B. In the villages studied, roof repairs were linked to fewer work interruptions, although other factors also mattered. | Passes. It preserves the study’s population, cautious relationship, and limitation. |

**Correct choice:** B.

Ask three scope questions:

- **Who?** Does the option refer to the same people or group?
- **When and where?** Does it remain within the passage’s setting and period?
- **How much?** Does it turn a limited result into a universal one?

## Step 4: Demand evidence for every important part

Many distractors contain one supported phrase and one unsupported addition. Because the first part looks familiar, students accept the entire option.

Break a choice into separate claims. If it says that X caused Y because of Z, test X, Y, causation, and Z independently. One unsupported major component can disqualify the option.

### Worked example: partial evidence

**Passage:**

> A library extended its opening hours during examination season. More students visited after 7 p.m., but the library did not collect data on whether the additional visits improved examination results.

**Question:** Which statement is supported by the passage?

| Option | Proof check |
|---|---|
| A. Extended hours improved students’ examination results by giving them more study time. | Fails. Neither improved results nor the proposed mechanism was measured. |
| B. Extended hours were followed by increased evening attendance, but their effect on examination results was not measured. | Passes. It states the observed change and retains the limitation. |

**Correct choice:** B.

Underline the option’s **load-bearing words**: *caused, because, therefore, best, only, more, less, always,* and *never*. These words often carry the unsupported part of the claim. Do not reject a choice merely because it contains a strong word; verify whether the passage supports that strength.

## Step 5: Measure inference distance

An inference is acceptable when it is a short, necessary step from the passage. It becomes risky when it requires several assumptions, outside facts, or a new chain of reasoning.

Consider the difference:

- **Short distance:** The passage says a policy reduced water use in one building; the option says the policy reduced water use in that building.
- **Long distance:** The same passage is used to claim that the policy will solve a citywide water shortage and should become mandatory.

### Worked example: unsupported leap

**Passage:**

> The town introduced a bicycle-sharing scheme near two railway stations. During the first six months, car trips from those stations declined modestly. The report did not examine whether former car users switched to bicycles or to buses.

**Question:** What can reasonably be inferred?

| Option | Proof check |
|---|---|
| A. The bicycle-sharing scheme caused every commuter to abandon cars. | Fails. It assumes definite causation, universal participation, and permanent behavioural change. |
| B. The scheme may have contributed to fewer car trips from the two stations, although the reasons for the decline remain uncertain. | Passes. It makes a cautious inference and retains the stated uncertainty. |

**Correct choice:** B.

For inference questions, ask: **Can I explain how the passage’s logic leads to this choice, or must I add facts the passage never gives me?**

## Step 6: Inspect wording precision

Small words can change an answer’s meaning. Pay attention to:

- absolute terms: *all, none, always, never, completely*;
- certainty terms: *proves, establishes, guarantees*;
- comparison terms: *more, less, most, only*;
- causal terms: *caused, led to, resulted in*;
- evaluative terms: *best, worst, unfair, useless*.

These are warning signals, not automatic proof of an incorrect answer. An absolute option may be correct if the passage genuinely makes an absolute claim. Reject it only when its strength exceeds the text’s support.

### Worked example: extreme wording

**Passage:**

> The author argues that remote work can improve concentration for tasks requiring long periods of uninterrupted attention. The passage also notes that collaborative projects may suffer when communication is poorly organised.

**Question:** What is the author’s attitude toward remote work?

| Option | Proof check |
|---|---|
| A. The author considers remote work universally superior to office work. | Fails. “Universally superior” erases the passage’s conditional benefit and its discussion of drawbacks. |
| B. The author presents remote work as advantageous for some tasks while acknowledging drawbacks in collaborative settings. | Passes. It reflects a balanced, conditional position. |

**Correct choice:** B. Tone questions are particularly vulnerable to distortion: an author can be favourable, sceptical, cautious, or mixed without being wholly supportive or hostile.

## Passage language is not proof by itself

An option may copy an exact phrase from the passage and still be wrong. Recycled language is evidence only when the option preserves the original meaning, scope, and relationship between ideas.

**Passage:** “The policy was introduced to reduce congestion, but its effect has not yet been measured.”

**Trap option:** “The policy reduced congestion.”

The option changes an intended purpose into a demonstrated result. It uses familiar language but fails the evidence check.

When a choice repeats passage wording, ask:

1. Is the phrase being used in the same context?
2. Has a possibility become a fact?
3. Has a purpose become an outcome?
4. Has a limited statement become a broad one?
5. Has a contrast or qualification been removed?

## When two options both seem correct

Do not compare which option sounds more polished. Compare which one requires fewer repairs.

1. **Match the question:** Which choice answers the exact task?
2. **Compare coverage:** Which includes the relevant central claim rather than an isolated detail?
3. **Preserve qualifications:** Which retains limits, uncertainty, and contrasts?
4. **Check scope:** Which adds fewer people, places, times, or conditions?
5. **Measure inference distance:** Which needs fewer assumptions?
6. **Prefer precision:** Which says exactly what the passage supports?

The strongest choice is not necessarily the longest or most nuanced. It is the one with the best textual proof and the fewest unsupported additions.

## A timed proof-before-pick checklist

Compress the method into six questions:

- **Task:** What is being asked—main idea, detail, inference, tone, or application?
- **Contradiction:** Does the option conflict with an explicit statement?
- **Scope:** Is it broader, stronger, or more certain than the passage?
- **Evidence:** Can I support every major clause?
- **Distance:** Does it require an outside assumption or several steps?
- **Precision:** Do words such as *only, always, caused,* or *best* exceed the evidence?

If a choice fails one **decisive** check, cross it out. A warning word or a vague feeling is not enough by itself; confirm the failure against the passage. This prevents both careless selection and over-elimination of a cautious but valid inference.

## Use an error log to improve validation

After practice, record the exact reason your choice failed instead of writing only “careless mistake.”

| Question | My choice | Correct choice | Failed check | Warning phrase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main idea | A | B | Question mismatch | “should be cancelled” |
| Inference | C | D | Scope expansion | “all users” |
| Detail | B | A | Unsupported addition | “because” |
| Tone | D | C | Overstated wording | “universally” |

Over time, this can reveal a recurring validation problem: accepting causal language too readily, overlooking scope changes, or rejecting reasonable inferences because they are not stated word for word. Correct the reasoning error, not merely the individual question.

## Does the method transfer across exams?

The proof-before-pick method is a **generalizable reasoning framework** for passage-based questions. It is not a verified official rule or guaranteed feature of every exam named in a preparation plan. Different tests may vary in passage length, question labels, timing, answer format, and the balance between literal comprehension and inference.

What remains useful across many passage-based exams is the underlying discipline: connect the answer to the passage, preserve its limits, and reject additions that the text cannot support. Apply the method alongside the instructions and question style of the specific examination you are taking.

## FAQ

### How can I find the correct answer in a reading comprehension passage?

First identify the question type. Then test each option for contradiction, scope, evidence, inference distance, and wording precision. Select the option that answers the exact question and requires the fewest unsupported assumptions.

### What makes a reading comprehension answer choice a trap?

A trap often sounds plausible but changes one important feature: it reverses the passage, broadens a limited claim, adds an unsupported reason, makes a cautious inference certain, or removes a qualification. It may also repeat passage language without preserving its meaning.

### Why can an option containing passage language still be incorrect?

Familiar words do not guarantee a familiar claim. The option may turn a purpose into an outcome, a possibility into a fact, or a limited observation into a universal conclusion.

### How much inference is acceptable in an RC answer?

A short inference is acceptable when the passage’s logic supports it. Be cautious when an option requires several unstated assumptions, outside knowledge, or a conclusion stronger than the evidence.

### What should I do when two reading comprehension options both seem correct?

Compare their proof. Check which one answers the exact question, covers the relevant passage, preserves qualifications, stays within scope, and uses fewer unsupported additions. The more precise option is usually stronger, but precision must be judged against the text rather than by length or style.

### Is outside knowledge always irrelevant?

No. But for a passage-based question, the selected answer must be justified primarily by the passage. Outside knowledge should not make you accept an option that the text contradicts or fails to support.

### How can I validate an answer quickly under exam time pressure?

Use the compressed checklist: task, contradiction, scope, evidence, distance, precision. Cross out an option only after identifying a decisive textual failure, then move on instead of repeatedly rereading an attractive distractor.
