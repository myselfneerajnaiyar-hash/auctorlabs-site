## Why agreement questions feel harder than they are

**Subject-Verb Agreement: The Grammar Mistakes That Trap Exam Takers** usually arise when a sentence places distracting words between the subject and its verb. The underlying rule is simple: a finite verb must agree with its subject in number and person.

- *The analyst **checks** the figures.*
- *The analysts **check** the figures.*

The challenge is identifying the subject accurately. A plural noun may appear next to a singular verb, or a singular noun ending in **-s** may look plural. In an error-spotting question, the goal is to locate the incorrect segment. In a sentence-correction question, you must choose the option that produces a grammatically sound sentence, sometimes while also considering clarity or concision.

The rules below teach formal, exam-oriented standard English. Collective nouns, quantities, and words such as **data** can vary by meaning, dialect, or style guide. Before publication or classroom use, a qualified English educator should review examples involving these borderline cases. Also check the current specification of your exam rather than assuming every pattern appears in every test.

## The core method: locate, isolate, match

Use this sequence instead of choosing the verb that sounds closest to the noun beside it.

### 1. Locate the finite verb

A finite verb carries tense or agreement. In *The results from the trial **suggest** a change*, **suggest** is the finite verb. In *The results **have been** unexpected*, **have** carries the agreement; **been** is part of the same verb phrase.

### 2. Find the grammatical subject

Ask: **Who or what is performing the action, or what is being described?** The answer is the subject.

> *The schedule [for the evening classes] **needs** revision.*

The subject is **schedule**, not **classes**.

### 3. Isolate interruptions

Temporarily remove prepositional phrases, parenthetical descriptions, and nonessential clauses. This exposes the central relationship:

> *The schedule ~~for the evening classes~~ **needs** revision.*

This is a checking technique, not a way to ignore meaning. Once you identify the agreement pattern, read the complete sentence again.

## Trap 1: Prepositional phrases between subject and verb

A prepositional phrase may begin with **of, with, in, from, among,** or **along with**. The noun inside it usually does not control the verb.

- *The collection **of rare maps is** stored upstairs.*
- *The instructions **for the new devices are** unclear.*
- *A group **of volunteers has** offered assistance.*
- *The director, along with two advisers, **has** approved the plan.*

In the first sentence, **collection** is singular. **Maps** is the object of **of**. In the third, **group** is the subject; **volunteers** is part of a modifying phrase.

A common exam error is to select **are** in *A group of volunteers are...* because **volunteers** is plural. Strip away the interruption: *A group has offered assistance.*

## Trap 2: Singular nouns ending in -s

Word endings are clues, not proof. Some singular nouns end in **-s**:

- *The news **is** encouraging.*
- *Mathematics **requires** careful reasoning.*
- *The analysis **shows** a flaw.*

Other **-s** nouns are plural:

- *The statistics **are** difficult to interpret.*
- *The premises **are** being inspected.*

Meaning and context matter. **Statistics** can refer to numerical data or to the academic field; different conventions may treat those meanings differently. In a formal question, use the meaning signalled by the sentence and the convention reflected in the answer choices.

Pair nouns such as **scissors, trousers,** and **spectacles** generally take plural verbs when referring to the objects:

- *The scissors **are** on the desk.*

With **a pair of**, the subject is **pair**:

- *A pair of scissors **is** on the desk.*

## Trap 3: Subjects joined by and, or, or nor

### Subjects joined by **and**

Two separate subjects joined by **and** normally take a plural verb:

- *The editor and the proofreader **review** every chapter.*

A singular verb is possible when **and** expresses one combined idea, one item, or one person with two roles:

- *Bread and butter **is** the café’s standard breakfast.*
- *The founder and chief designer **has** approved the layout.*

The second sentence describes one person. If it describes two people, write: *The founder and the chief designer **have** approved the layout.* The repeated article **the** can help signal that two distinct people are intended, although context remains decisive.

### Subjects joined by **or** or **nor**

In formal standard usage, the verb generally agrees with the nearer subject:

- *Neither the assistants nor the manager **was** available.*
- *Neither the manager nor the assistants **were** available.*

Do not let **neither** automatically force a singular verb. Identify the subject nearest to the verb. In careful writing, rewriting may be clearer: *The manager and the assistants were unavailable.*

## Trap 4: Indefinite pronouns

Indefinite pronouns are frequent sources of agreement errors because the noun after **of** looks more prominent than the pronoun that actually controls the verb.

### Usually singular

**Each, every, everyone, everybody, either, neither, anyone, anybody, someone, somebody, no one,** and **nobody** generally take singular verbs.

- *Each of the proposals **contains** a cost estimate.*
- *Everyone in the workshops **has** received a badge.*
- *Neither of the explanations **is** convincing.*

The subjects are **each, everyone,** and **neither**. The nouns after **of** do not control agreement.

### Usually plural

**Both, few, many,** and **several** take plural verbs:

- *Several of the entries **need** correction.*
- *Both of the routes **lead** to the station.*

### Dependent on the following noun

**All, any, more, most, none,** and **some** can take singular or plural verbs depending on what they refer to:

- *Some of the equipment **is** damaged.*
- *Some of the instruments **are** damaged.*
- *Most of the work **has** been completed.*
- *Most of the assignments **have** been graded.*

For **none**, both singular and plural agreement can be defensible. Use singular when the meaning is close to “not one” and plural when the meaning is close to “not any,” while also following the convention expected by the test.

## Trap 5: Collective nouns and intended meaning

A collective noun names a group: **team, committee, family, audience, staff, government, jury,** or **class**.

Use singular agreement when the group acts as one unit:

- *The committee **has** issued its recommendation.*
- *The jury **has** reached a decision.*

Plural agreement may occur when the sentence focuses on individual members, particularly in varieties of English that permit notional plural agreement:

- *The committee **are** divided over the proposal.*

For formal exam writing, singular agreement is usually the safer convention when the group is presented as a single decision-making body. However, do not apply that mechanically. The phrase **are divided** clearly highlights separate members. If the question gives no useful context, inspect the answer choices and the style of English being tested.

## Trap 6: Quantities, fractions, percentages, and data

### Measurements and amounts

A measurement viewed as one total amount often takes a singular verb:

- *Eight kilometres **is** too far for a short break.*
- *Three hours **is** enough for the examination.*

When the quantity refers to separate units or a period that has elapsed, a plural verb may be more natural:

- *Three hours **have** passed since the session began.*

The intended meaning determines the agreement.

### Fractions and percentages

A fraction or percentage usually agrees with the noun after **of**:

- *Half of the equipment **is** unused.*
- *Half of the machines **are** unused.*
- *Thirty percent of the budget **has** been allocated.*
- *Thirty percent of the applicants **have** withdrawn.*

Do not choose a plural verb merely because the percentage is large. Identify the noun being measured.

### The noun **data**

Formal exam conventions may treat **data** as a plural noun:

- *The data **suggest** a change in the pattern.*

Contemporary professional usage often treats **data** as a mass noun:

- *The data **suggests** a change in the pattern.*

Neither form should be declared universally correct without considering context, variety, and style guide. In a test question, follow the convention established by the passage or answer choices. If you are writing an unambiguous practice item, use a less disputed noun such as **evidence** or **results**.

### **There** and **here** constructions

In existential constructions, **there** and **here** are not the true subjects. Look for the noun after the verb:

- *There **is** a problem in the final paragraph.*
- *There **are** several problems in the final paragraph.*
- *Here **are** the documents you requested.*

## Trap 7: Inverted sentences and questions

Questions and sentences beginning with negative adverbs can reverse normal word order.

- *Where **are** the archived files?* The subject is **files**.
- *Rarely **does** the laboratory accept incomplete samples.* The subject is **laboratory**.
- *Only after the interviews **was** the decision announced.* The subject is **decision**.

In *Does the report contain errors?*, **report** is the subject. The auxiliary **does** carries the singular agreement, so the main verb remains in its base form: **contain**, not **contains**.

## Trap 8: Relative clauses

In a relative clause, **who, which,** or **that** takes its number from its antecedent—the noun it refers to.

- *The analyst who **reviews** the figures checks every total.*
- *The analysts who **review** the figures check every total.*
- *The report that **explains** the changes is available.*
- *The reports that **explain** the changes are available.*

The nearby noun inside the clause can distract you. In *The analyst who reviews the figures checks...*, **who** refers to **analyst**, so **reviews** is singular. The main verb **checks** also agrees with **analyst**.

Structures such as *the only one of the researchers who works...* can be ambiguous. **Who** may be understood as referring to **one** or, in some readings, to **researchers**. For timed practice, avoid relying on that sentence unless the wider context makes the intended reference unmistakable.

## Worked examples: eliminate the tempting answer

### Example 1: intervening phrase

*The set of revised instructions **include/includes** a safety diagram.*

**Answer: includes.** The subject is **set**. The phrase **of revised instructions** is an interruption, so the singular verb is required.

**Why the distractor fails:** **Include** matches **instructions**, the nearest noun, but that noun is not the subject.

### Example 2: neither...nor

*Neither the coordinator nor the volunteers **was/were** informed about the change.*

**Answer: were.** The nearer subject is plural **volunteers**.

**Why the distractor fails:** **Was** may seem attractive because **neither** is usually singular, but the paired structure uses the nearer subject for agreement in this formal convention.

### Example 3: existential construction

*There **is/are** several reasons for the delay.*

**Answer: are.** **There** is an introductory word, not the subject. The subject is **several reasons**, which is plural.

**Why the distractor fails:** The sentence begins with **there**, but opening position does not determine agreement.

### Example 4: inverted question

*Why **does/do** the revised instructions appear unclear?*

**Answer: do.** The subject is plural **instructions**. The auxiliary must be **do**, and the main verb remains **appear**.

**Why the distractor fails:** **Does** would require a singular subject, such as *Why does the revised instruction appear unclear?*

### Example 5: collective noun

*The panel **has/have** reached a unanimous decision.*

**Answer: has.** The panel is acting as one body, signalled by **a unanimous decision**.

**Why the distractor fails:** **Have** would be more plausible if the sentence described separate members, as in *The panel have disagreed among themselves* in a convention that permits plural collective agreement.

## Mini-practice set

Choose the better verb.

1. The catalogue of archived files **needs/need** updating.
2. Every one of the candidates **has/have** submitted an identification form.
3. Most of the equipment **was/were** replaced.
4. The analysts who review the figures **checks/check** each total.
5. Neither the supervisors nor the assistant **was/were** present.
6. There **is/are** two possible explanations.
7. The class **has/have** completed the exercise as a single group.
8. Where **is/are** the corrected answer sheets?

### Answers and explanations

1. **needs** — **catalogue** is singular; **of archived files** is a prepositional phrase.
2. **has** — **every one** is singular; **candidates** does not control the verb.
3. **was** — **equipment** is an uncountable singular noun.
4. **check** — **who** refers to plural **analysts**.
5. **was** — **assistant**, the nearer subject, is singular.
6. **are** — The post-verbal subject is plural **two explanations**.
7. **has** — The class is presented as one group completing one exercise.
8. **are** — The subject is plural **answer sheets**, not **where**.

## A fast decision process for timed questions

When time is limited, use this compact routine:

1. **Circle the finite verb or auxiliary.**
2. **Ask who or what it describes.**
3. **Cross out phrases beginning with words such as of, with, or in.**
4. **Check special structures:** and, or, nor, indefinite pronouns, relative clauses, and collective nouns.
5. **If there or here comes first, inspect the noun after the verb.**
6. **Read the sentence for meaning before finalising the answer.**

A sentence that feels unfamiliar is not necessarily incorrect. Confirm the subject, the verb form, and the intended meaning before marking an error.

## FAQ

### What is subject-verb agreement?

It is the matching of a finite verb with its subject in number and person. Singular subjects generally take singular verbs, and plural subjects generally take plural verbs.

### Why is the nearest noun often a distraction?

A modifier or prepositional phrase can place a noun between the real subject and the verb. The nearest noun may therefore be grammatically unrelated to the agreement decision.

### Which nouns ending in -s can take a singular verb?

Words such as **news, mathematics,** and **analysis** can be singular despite their spelling. Determine grammatical number from meaning, not from the final letter alone.

### Are collective nouns always singular?

No. Use singular agreement when the group acts as one unit. Plural agreement may be appropriate when individual members are emphasised, depending on the variety of English and the convention being tested.

### How should I handle fractions and percentages?

Look at the noun after **of**. *Forty percent of the land is protected* uses singular **land**, while *forty percent of the parcels are protected* uses plural **parcels**.

### How do error-spotting and sentence-correction questions differ?

Error spotting asks you to locate the faulty part, while sentence correction asks you to select a revised version. In both formats, identify the subject before judging the verb; correction questions may additionally reward clarity or standard construction.

### How can I distinguish an agreement error from an unfamiliar sentence?

Reduce the sentence to its subject and finite verb, check the relevant rule, and then restore the removed phrases. If the subject and verb agree after that process, the unusual wording may be acceptable rather than erroneous.
